Guía del profesor · Teacher guide (≈ 55 min)

Pacing guide: Assessment · All modes · Grammar: — · Product: Unit 5 IPA: interpretive reading + interpersonal conversation

  1. Instrucciones (3 min): Read the instructions and the poll. Remind students: no devices other than the lesson screen, no translators.
  2. Lectura (10 min): Students read silently. They may tap glossed words. Do NOT open the teacher translation. Optionally play the audio once for the whole class.
  3. Comprensión (8 min): Students choose answers on paper or on screen. Reveal only after everyone finishes (or after collecting papers).
  4. Interpretación (9 min): Inference MC, then the short written response. Collect the written responses.
  5. Interpersonal (20 min): Pairs draw a role card; 3–4 minutes per conversation, unrehearsed. Score with the rubric while other students work on an extension task (written response or reading again).
  6. Reflexión (5 min): Students self-rate against the unit Can-Dos and set one goal for the midterm.

Because the class is large, run the interpersonal conversations in pairs at a side table while the rest of the class completes the interpretive tasks; continue on Day 96 warm-up if needed. Una semana sin pantallas: el experimento de un colegio en Medellín

El mes pasado, los estudiantes de undécimo grado de un colegio público de Medellín, Colombia, aceptaron un reto poco común: pasar una semana sin redes sociales. La idea fue de Marcela Ortiz, la profesora de ciencias sociales, porque notó que sus estudiantes llegaban a clase cansados y distraídos. «No quiero que mis estudiantes odien la tecnología», explicó. «Quiero que piensen en cómo la usan».

Las reglas eran sencillas. Los estudiantes podían usar el teléfono para llamar a su familia, escuchar música y hacer la tarea, pero no podían abrir aplicaciones como Instagram o TikTok. Cada noche escribían en un diario cómo se sentían.

Los primeros días fueron difíciles. «El lunes revisé mi teléfono como veinte veces sin pensar», cuenta Samuel, de dieciséis años. «Me sentía aislado porque mis amigos hablaban de videos que yo no conocía». Pero a mitad de semana, algo cambió. Varios estudiantes dijeron que dormían mejor y que tenían más tiempo para sus pasatiempos. Laura, por ejemplo, volvió a pintar, algo que no hacía desde niña.

Al final de la semana, la clase organizó una discusión. No todos estaban de acuerdo. Algunos estudiantes creen que el experimento les ha cambiado la vida; otros piensan que las redes sociales son necesarias para estar informados y mantenerse en contacto con familiares que viven lejos. Samuel ha decidido borrar una aplicación, pero no todas. «Las redes no son el enemigo», dice. «El problema es cuando ellas deciden por ti cuánto tiempo pasas en línea».

Ahora la profesora Ortiz recomienda que otras escuelas hagan el experimento, pero con una condición: que los estudiantes participen voluntariamente. «Si los obligamos, no aprenden nada», dice. «Quiero que cada persona descubra su propio equilibrio».

Last month, the eleventh-grade students at a public school in Medellín, Colombia, accepted an unusual challenge: spending a week without social media. The idea came from Marcela Ortiz, the social studies teacher, because she noticed that her students were arriving to class tired and distracted. "I don't want my students to hate technology," she explained. "I want them to think about how they use it."

The rules were simple. Students could use their phones to call their families, listen to music and do homework, but they couldn't open apps like Instagram or TikTok. Every night they wrote in a journal about how they felt.

The first days were hard. "On Monday I checked my phone about twenty times without thinking," says Samuel, 16. "I felt isolated because my friends were talking about videos I didn't know." But by midweek, something changed. Several students said they were sleeping better and had more time for their hobbies. Laura, for example, started painting again, something she hadn't done since she was a child.

At the end of the week, the class held a discussion. Not everyone agreed. Some students believe the experiment has changed their lives; others think social media is necessary to stay informed and keep in touch with relatives who live far away. Samuel has decided to delete one app, but not all of them. "Social media isn't the enemy," he says. "The problem is when it decides for you how much time you spend online."

Now Ms. Ortiz recommends that other schools try the experiment, but with one condition: that students participate voluntarily. "If we force them, they don't learn anything," she says. "I want each person to discover their own balance."
