Come on in! The market · Unit 4: Health and Wellness · Markets in Mexico and Peru vs. the supermarket

Guía del profesor · Teacher guide (≈ 55 min)

Pacing guide: Culture & Comparison · Presentational · Grammar: Comparatives review · Product: Comparison chart

  1. Gancho (7 min): Poll, then the vocabulary cards with audio. Ask: Who has been to a farmers' market, a bodega or Haymarket in Boston? Thumbs up / sideways / down.
  2. Lectura (11 min): Read the two posts aloud once (audio), then students reread silently and tap glosses. 4 MC items whole-class with mini-whiteboards.
  3. Comparación (7 min): Venn sort in pairs; the pair that finishes first explains two cards to the class.
  4. 3 P (7 min): Walk through products / practices / perspectives. Ask students to add one more example to each column on paper.
  5. Repaso: comparar (7 min): Fill-ins individually; review items 9–10 (pronouns from Day 72) together.
  6. Discusión (6 min): Roulette in groups of 3 with a 2-minute timer; each answer must include a comparative.
  7. Párrafo (7 min): Students start the comparison paragraph with the frame; finish as homework if needed. This is today's product (comparison chart + paragraph).
  8. Boleto de salida (3 min): MC + confidence.

Recycles comparatives from Days 51–52 and the double object pronouns from Day 72 (¿Me lo da? — Sí, se lo doy). Keep the comparison respectful: different, not better/worse. Optional extension: students compare prices or products at a local bodega or a farmers' market. Haymarket (Boston) is an open-air produce market (Fridays and Saturdays, on Blackstone Street near the North End) with roots in the early 1800s — a useful local connection.

Idea clave

En México y Perú, mucha gente compra frutas, verduras, carne y pan en el mercado del barrio, varias veces por semana. Los mercados también tienen fondas o puestos de comida donde se desayuna y se almuerza.

In Mexico and Peru, many people buy fruit, vegetables, meat and bread at the neighborhood market several times a week. Markets also have food stalls where people eat breakfast and lunch.

Mi mercado favorito

Ximena, 17 años, Oaxaca (México): Los sábados acompaño a mi abuela al mercado. Ella no hace una lista: camina por los pasillos, mira, huele y prueba. «Pruebe esta mandarina, joven», me dicen, y yo la pruebo. Los vendedores llaman a los clientes: «¡Pásele, pásele! ¿Qué le damos?». Mi abuela tiene su vendedora favorita de tomates, doña Chela, que siempre le guarda los más rojos y, a veces, le da un pilón: unos chiles o un poco de cilantro. En el pasillo de humo, eliges la carne y alguien te la asa allí mismo. El mercado es más ruidoso que un supermercado, pero también es más divertido.

Rodrigo, 16 años, Lima (Perú): En mi casa compramos casi todo en el mercado de Surquillo. Mi mamá dice que las frutas son más frescas que en el supermercado y que hay tantas variedades de papa que no las puedes contar. Allí descubrí la lúcuma, la chirimoya y la granadilla. Mi mamá es casera de la señora Rosa: la saluda, le pregunta por su familia y la señora Rosa le da la yapa aunque mi mamá no se la pide. Casi nunca regateamos por la comida; regatear es más común en los mercados de artesanía. En el supermercado todo es más rápido y está envasado, pero nadie te pregunta cómo estás.

¿Y tú? En Massachusetts, ¿compras en un supermercado, en una bodega o en un mercado como Haymarket, en Boston? ¿Conoces a las personas que te venden la comida?

Haz clic en las palabras subrayadas para ver su significado.

Ximena, 17, Oaxaca (Mexico): On Saturdays I go with my grandmother to the market. She doesn't make a list: she walks through the aisles, looks, smells and tastes. «Try this tangerine, young lady», they tell me, and I try it. The vendors call to customers: «Come in, come in! What can we get you?». My grandmother has her favorite tomato vendor, Doña Chela, who always saves her the reddest ones and sometimes gives her a little extra: a few chiles or some cilantro. In the «smoke aisle», you choose the meat and someone grills it for you right there. The market is noisier than a supermarket, but it's also more fun.

Rodrigo, 16, Lima (Peru): At home we buy almost everything at the Surquillo market. My mom says the fruit is fresher than at the supermarket and that there are so many varieties of potatoes you can't count them. There I discovered lúcuma, chirimoya and granadilla. My mom is a regular of Mrs. Rosa: she greets her, asks about her family, and Mrs. Rosa gives her the yapa even though my mom doesn't ask her for it. We almost never bargain for food; bargaining is more common at craft markets. At the supermarket everything is faster and packaged, but nobody asks you how you are.

And you? In Massachusetts, do you shop at a supermarket, a bodega or a market like Haymarket in Boston? Do you know the people who sell you your food?

Ojo con las generalizaciones: también hay supermercados grandes en Lima y en Ciudad de México, y en Massachusetts hay mercados al aire libre, bodegas y mercados de agricultores. Usa palabras como muchas veces, en general, en muchos lugares.

Avoid overgeneralizing: there are big supermarkets in Lima and Mexico City, and there are open-air, bodega and farmers' markets in Massachusetts. Use hedges like «often», «in general», «in many places».

Idea clave

La perspectiva explica la práctica: si valoras la relación con tu casera, compras en su puesto, aunque el supermercado está más cerca.

The perspective explains the practice: if you value your relationship with your regular vendor, you buy from her even if the supermarket is closer.

Compare a market in Mexico or Peru with where your family buys food in Massachusetts: one similarity, two differences, one perspective. Use comparatives.
